Valve Turning Design

What to build before the hardware turn: where to stick the motion-capture markers on the valve's rotor, how to give its bearing the right amount of resistance, and a handle that lets the clamped claw roll.

1. Marker layout

Top view, for placing them with a ruler

All positions are millimetres from the centre of the axis hole. Measure x along the beam toward the fin and y across it from the beam's centre line. Stand at the hub end and look toward the fin: +y is on your left.

Side view, with the drone's clearance

Positions and distances

Motive tells the markers apart by their distances to each other, so no two may be equal. The highlighted rows are the closest pair of distances. Heights are marker centres above the table, assuming 4 mm bases and 12.7 mm spheres.

Fitting them

On the rotor

  1. Mark the beam's centre line with a pencil from the axis hole to the bracket.
  2. Measure each marker's x along that line from the hole's centre, then its y across it. M2 sits on a 30 mm post; glue the post square to the beam.
  3. Every base has at least 2.5 mm of flat top around it, so a marker placed within 2 mm of its spot still sits flat. Bases up to 17 mm across also fit.
  4. Keep everything off the bracket from x = 135 mm outward: the fin, the claw's jaws and the bracket's screws are there.
  5. For the 5-marker plan, fit M1 to M4 exactly as in the 4-marker plan and add M5. Nothing moves.

In Motive

  1. Turn the valve so the beam points along the room's +x, fin toward +x, then create the rigid body from the markers. Its x axis then lies along the beam.
  2. Name it obj_0.
  3. Move the pivot to the bearing axis at table level. To find the axis, turn the beam slowly through a quarter turn or more: each marker traces a circle around it.
  4. Turn the valve through a full revolution and watch for a flipped or jumping solution. There should be none.

The planner reads the valve's axis and beam angle from obj_0. A pivot 5 mm off puts the claw 5 mm off the fin.

2. Adding friction to the bearing

The sealed 608 bearing measured under 32 mN·m and is probably a few mN·m. In the simulation a frictionless valve failed 2 of 3 turns, so if the bearing is below about 5 mN·m, add resistance on purpose. What the simulation flew:

Constant friction
10–40 mN·m
0.06–0.25 N at the fin. Turns completed from 5 to 48 mN·m. A hanging weight adding a constant 100 mN·m made it worse.
Speed-dependent damping
≈ 0.5 N·m·s/rad
The best single turn on record (one flight). 2.0 aborted on the claw-force guard.
Adding weight
0.06 mN·m per kg
A ball bearing's friction barely depends on load, so stacking weight does not work. Weight off the axis only adds inertia.

Option A: two spring-loaded felt pads on the hub

Two pads on printed brackets screwed to the base plate pinch the hub's exposed side from opposite directions, so the bearing sees no side load. The hub is exposed in a 20 mm band between the plate top (20 mm) and the beam's underside (40 mm), and the beam sweeps over everything outside the hub, so keep the brackets below about 36 mm, a few millimetres under the beam. A screw compressing a small spring behind each pad sets the force.

Use felt or PTFE, not rubber. Rubber grips harder at rest than in motion, so the beam would stick and then jump, and every jump is a jolt on the claw.

Option B: a grease-filled sleeve around the hub

A printed sleeve stands up from a new base plate around the hub, leaving a thin gap filled with silicone damping grease or very thick silicone fluid. The film shears as the valve turns, so the resistance grows with speed and is zero at rest. This is the kind of resistance the simulation liked best.

Silicone fluids thin with temperature and shear, so treat the number as a starting point and measure what you build. An off-the-shelf rotary damper through a 3:1 gear step-up (about 12 N·cm at 20 rpm on the damper side) gives the same behaviour without the mess. The two options can be combined: a few mN·m of felt so the beam never free-wheels, plus damping.

3. Measuring the resistance

At the fin the target friction is only 0.06–0.25 N, too small for a 10 N spring gauge. Measure on the hub instead. A thread wound round the hub (r = 25 mm) needs 6.4 times the force for the same torque, and a hanging weight read on a kitchen scale gives the torque to about 0.25 mN·m. Measure the bare bearing first. Fit the felt pads only if it reads under 5 mN·m, then measure again while setting their screws.

Reading to torque

Steps

  1. Wind 2–3 turns of strong thread (fishing line) round the hub just above the plate, at about 22 mm, and tape the end to the hub. With the felt pads fitted, the winding must stay below them; their bottom is at 24 mm.
  2. Clamp a small ball-bearing pulley to the table edge beside the C-clamp, its top level with the winding. It must stay below 38 mm so the beam passes over it. Lead the thread straight along the side of the plate, over the pulley, and hang a light cup from it.
  3. Starting friction: from rest, add weight in 5 g steps until the valve starts to turn by itself.
  4. Running friction, the value that matters for the turn: take weight off until the valve, nudged by hand, stops by itself. The smallest weight that keeps a nudged valve turning is the running friction.
  5. Repeat at three beam angles a quarter turn apart, and in the other direction (wind the thread the other way round and move the pulley to the other end of the plate).
  6. Pulley check: hang equal cups on both ends of the thread over the pulley alone and add weight to one side until it moves. Subtract that from every reading.
  7. Torque = (weight − pulley weight) × 9.81 × 0.025 m. The target band is 41–163 g (10–40 mN·m). Under 20 g (5 mN·m) the bearing is in the weak range: fit the pads and set the screws until the running friction reads about 80 g.

With the spring gauge instead

No pulley needed: hook the gauge on the hub thread and pull it level along the side of the plate, slowly and steadily, reading while the valve turns. Torque = reading × 0.025 m. Each 0.1 N division is 2.5 mN·m, so the target band reads 0.4–1.6 N.

Cross-check with mocap

  1. Record the markers: ros2 bag record /vrpn_mocap/obj_0/pose.
  2. Flick the beam to about half a turn to one turn per second and let it coast to a stop. Repeat 5 times, from different angles.
  3. Friction torque = rotor inertia × deceleration. The rotor's inertia is about 2.6 × 10⁻³ kg·m², plus about 2 % for the markers.

This measures the friction while turning and shows whether it grows with speed. Its accuracy rests on the inertia estimate, about ±5–10 %, so trust the weights for the number.

Grease sleeve or damper

The sleeve covers the hub and a damper has no resistance at rest, so the weights cannot measure it. Pull the fin with the gauge, at right angles to the beam, at a steady pace while mocap records the beam angle.

Damping = (gauge × 0.160 m − running friction) ÷ turning speed in rad/s. At 0.5 N·m·s/rad the gauge reads about 0.7 N at 12°/s (the fin moving 33 mm/s) and 1.4 N at 25°/s (70 mm/s).

4. A handle the claw can roll on

With the jaws clamped on the fin, the arm's four joints are used up. Three hold the claw's position and the wrist turns the claw about its own axis. The claw's two other rotations are then set by how the drone moves, so the pads, the fin and the printed shaft take them and push back on the drone through the arm. On 10-29 that coupling grew a 0.45 Hz sway until the arm buckled, 6.9 s after the grasp. A pin on the handle that lets the clamped claw roll removes the coupling.

The pin frees the claw; it does not add damping. Once the claw can roll, the drone gets its free-flight damping back (the 10-28 reduced model returns the sideways mode to its free-flight decay). A damper at the pin would put the coupling back: at the sway's 0.5 rad/s it has to pass under 5 mN·m, so below 0.01 N·m·s/rad.

Handle
0°
paddle (swings with the claw) post and bearing housing (fixed) pin, steel weight gripper, simplified pin axis

Which way the pin points

Roll and sideways sway both turn the clamped claw about one axis. It lies in the fin's plane, square to the claw's axis: 30° above horizontal, rising toward the turn direction (+y). A pin along it frees both. A horizontal pin, along the fin's width, frees only the part along the tangent, about half the angle. Pitch, fore-aft and vertical motion turn the claw about the jaws' closing axis instead. The pin leaves those alone, but they matter less: the drone holds its height to about ±5 mm, and the valve itself turns to follow the fore-aft motion.

Grip rotation forced by each drone motion at the flown pose, with the valve held by its friction (wb_valve_turning_flight_20261029/tools/handle_joint_mobility.py). The flights agree: 0.10–0.15° of claw roll per mm of sideways sway, and 4.5° rms of claw-against-fin rotation about the tangent on 10-29. The axis stays within 5° of 30° over the arm poses the turn visits.

Where the pin goes

Through the grip point G, the point the planner aims the claw at: mid-width on the fin, 25 mm below its top (x 160, y 0.6, z 179 mm). If the pin misses G by a distance d, the claw has to move d·θ to roll by θ, and the law's task spring (120 N/m) resists that like a torsion spring of 120·d². Within 10 mm this is negligible. A hinge at the fin's base, 105 mm below G, would be stiffer than the grip it is meant to free.

The layout

The fin comes off. A paddle with the same grip area hangs from a 4 mm steel pin through G. The pin is fixed in a boss on the paddle's −y edge and turns in two miniature ball bearings in a post on the claw's side of the root. The pin line falls toward −y, so the post stays low: its top is 11 mm below G, under the gripper, which rises toward the wrist on that side. The fingertips point +y and down, away from it.

What the joint needs

Parts

  • Paddle, printed: 40 × 80 × 6 mm in the old fin's top 80 mm (y −19.4 to 20.6, z 124 to 204), a Ø12 boss along the pin on its −y edge, a pocket for the steel weight at the bottom, two stop bumps and a Ø3 lock hole on the boss's flange.
  • Post, printed with a new rotor (the root widened to y −47) or screwed to the root with two M3: a 12 × 12 mm column at y −47 to −35 up to z 156, carrying a Ø16 housing along the pin.
  • Pin: Ø4 × 50 mm stainless dowel, or an M4 shoulder screw with a 4 mm shank. Glued or set-screwed into the boss.
  • 2 × MR84ZZ bearings (4 × 8 × 3 mm), a 4 mm shim washer between the boss and the near bearing, a 4 mm E-clip behind the far one.
  • About 10 g of steel in the paddle's bottom (an M6 nut and washer).
  • A Ø3 dowel that locks the paddle to the post, for the locked/free comparison.

Clearances

The OM-X fingers are taken as 20 mm wide, reaching 40 mm back toward the wrist and 20 mm past G. Check them on the real gripper. The 20 mm case is the Valve Turning tab's close gate.

What it fixes

  • The roll coupling behind the 10-28 and 10-29 contact sway, for roll and sideways motion: about 99 % of the forced rotation at the flown pose.
  • The turn itself is unchanged. The push passes through the pin's axis, so it turns the valve as the fin did, and the grip point, the planner's offsets and obj_0 stay where they are.

What it does not

  • The wrist kick when the jaws open (24–41° on 10-28/29): open them earlier.
  • The valve following the drone's fore-aft wander: that is the free-axis anchor's share, by design.
  • The airframe's yaw imbalance (0.2 N·m standing trim): re-true the frame.
  • Isaac does not reproduce the contact sway, so only a flight can show the gain. Isaac can check the grasp and the turn once the asset has the joint.

Testing it

  1. On the bench: with the claw clamped by hand, the paddle turns ±20° with no felt resistance and hangs back within a few degrees when let go. With the lock dowel in, it does not move.
  2. In flight, on one battery: a 10 s clamped hold with no turn, locked, then free. Compare the CoM error rate (the planner's new sway guard aborts above 0.15 m/s) and the claw's roll from the arm's joints.
  3. Then the 90° turn, free.

For a −90° turn, mirror the whole handle: the post on +y, the pin rising toward −y.
