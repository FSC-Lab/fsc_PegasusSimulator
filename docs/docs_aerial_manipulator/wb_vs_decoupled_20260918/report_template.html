1. The arm-forward (x) settling is not a gain problem

After a 0.5 m base step along the arm the centre-of-mass error keeps a ~0.15 Hz, lightly damped oscillation and is still above 20 mm at the end of a 16 s hold; the lateral step settles under 20 mm in about 5 s. Twelve flights changed one parameter each (controller, observer and feed-forward filter):

The candidate was then flown on the demo trajectory itself, and it loses there. The table above is four half-metre steps; the demo is a tracked circle, and the two rank differently — a slower translational observer buys the arm axis and pays for it on the lateral one, and a circle drives both axes every lap:

{{TUNE_TABLE}}

Worse on every column, at three and a half times the tilt. The shipped configuration is the best tuned result for the demo; nothing was changed.

The loop is at its delay margin: any gain increase destabilises it, less attitude damping is worse on both axes, and the only lever that moves the arm-axis mode (the translational observer bandwidth) moves the lateral axis the other way. Every trajectory run below shows the same signature, a ~1.1–1.4 s effective closed-loop lag, so the shipped configuration was left unchanged. Reducing the lag needs a model change (an observer that predicts the rotor-lagged applied wrench, or a reference lead in the planner), not a gain, and that is the recommended follow-up after the flight test.

2. Cruise speed: error scales with speed, so the speed is a budget

Whole-body rig, 0.75 m circle at three lap times and the 1.5 × 0.75 m figure-8 at its planner-feasible maximum (bounded by the 0.3 rad/s yaw-rate limit: the airframe yaws once per lap), long axis on world x and on world y:

{{SWEEP_TABLE}}

The mean end-effector error is speed × a lag of 1.1–1.4 s, with a 35–70 mm residual after the lag is removed, so the cruise speed is a budget rather than an optimum. The circle at 0.10 m/s (48 s lap) and the figure-8 at 0.064 m/s (67 s lap, the planner's maximum under its 0.3 rad/s yaw-rate bound) keep the error near 100 mm; the 0.22 m/s circle doubles it.

Figure-8 orientation. The planner anchors the lemniscate by its start tangent along the vehicle's nose, and that tangent is 45° off the long axis, so takeoff headings 0° and 90° put the long axis on the diagonals (−45°, +45°: 91 vs 88 mm) and headings 45° and 135° put it on world x and world y. {{ORIENT_TEXT}}

What the comparison shows

  • End-effector position: the whole-body law is better on every run, by 10–23 % — circle 48 s: 121 vs 138 mm mean; figure-8 (+45° diagonal): 88 vs 98 mm; circle 24 s: 202 vs 248 mm. Both errors are dominated by the same closed-loop lag (1.1–1.3 s whole-body, 1.4–1.5 s decoupled); once the lag is fitted out the residuals are alike (29–52 mm decoupled, 34–69 mm whole-body).
  • End-effector heading: the whole-body law halves the error — 5.0° vs 10.3° (circle 48 s), 4.8° vs 11.5° (figure-8), 9.4° vs 20.3° (circle 24 s). The decoupled law's heading error is its base yaw error (10.3°, 11.5°, 20.3° — identical numbers): it runs the paper's constant-yaw specialization with no yaw-rate feed-forward, so the airframe lags the turning reference and the arm, holding its joints to 0.2–0.9° rms, carries that lag straight to the gripper. The whole-body law's yaw error is half (5.2°, 6.1°, 9.8°) and its arm moves 1.2–3.9° rms against its joint reference to keep the gripper on task.
  • Base position follows the same ordering (129 vs 156 mm, 100 vs 120 mm, 214 vs 280 mm). The decoupled law also holds altitude 11 mm low on every run (its L1 thrust estimate settling against the +17.6 % allocator error) where the whole-body observer holds it within 0.4–1.6 mm.
  • Joint tracking is the decoupled rig's strength (0.2–0.9° rms position servo vs 1.2–3.9° for the torque-mode arm under the whole-body law): its arm reproduces the planned joint trajectory almost exactly, which is precisely why its gripper inherits every base error. The whole-body law spends joint error to buy end-effector accuracy.
  • Neither saturated or tilted beyond 2.5°; both took off on the same SAFETY cascade, which on this +10 % plant swings ~1.5 m sideways before settling (pre-existing, both rigs, before the stability gate).

4. The safety guard during the trajectory

On a trip the vehicle holds station where it is. It does not land. The revert re-seeds the position reference to the pose of that instant, PX4 regains attitude, rate and mixing, the trajectory planner goes silent, and the arm folds home one second later, so the arm's reaction torque arrives after the attitude is back. Landing belongs to a different failsafe: only a loss of position feedback descends, at 0.2 m/s, holding x and y — and if the flight controller's own odometry is stale too, it does not descend at all.

The guard is armed throughout the tracked trajectory by construction: tilt and body rate are checked at the top of every inner-loop tick, and the drift check runs in the same tick after the reference is chosen, so it measures tracking error against the streamed plan. A planned half-metre move is not drift; only losing the vehicle is.

The guard is a fallback, not a rescue. It fired exactly as designed in all three, but the third vehicle had already been in a 12–20° attitude limit cycle for 95 seconds and was rotating at 96°/s when the limit was reached; nothing recovers that within a metre of the floor. That argues for keeping the tilt limit low rather than raising it, and for never starting a run on a vehicle that has not settled. A trip also cannot be forced mid-trajectory by lowering a threshold: the entry transient (0.4–0.9 m, 6–7°) is larger than anything the tracked run produces (0.13–0.19 m, 1.5°), which is the right ordering — it is why there is no false trip during the demo — but it makes entry, not the run, the sizing case.

The decoupled controller had tilt, rate and feedback-loss guards but no drift guard, so a vehicle walking away level would have tripped nothing. It now has the same one, default off everywhere else, set to 0.75 m here — four times the worst base error measured on the flown circle.

5. What changed in the repositories

  • Decoupled rig moved onto the whole-body plant. Its launcher runs the whole-body Isaac script with the arm in a new position-command mode (servo emulation through the same servo model); the plant knobs come from its own controller yaml, whose reality-model section is a copy of the whole-body one. Allocator thrust belief and CoM error matched; the SAFETY block aligned (the ±2 N UDE bound could not lift the +10 % plant — it stalled at 0.94 m).
  • One planner, two consumers. The decoupled stack launches the same trajectory planner from the whole-body yaml, and a reference bridge converts its stream for the decoupled law. The controller's reference input is remapped so the converted stream cannot re-enter the planner as a target (it did, on the first attempt).
  • Position-mode arm tracks a streamed trajectory. The position controller gained pure-tracking mode; the arm planner owns the reference in SAFETY, the trajectory planner in DIRECT.
  • Ground stations. The arm station's EE Trajectory tab shows which controller its operations land in (Whole-Body / Decoupled / Baseline); the drone station lists Baseline, Decoupled and Whole-Body.
